About

Reading landscapes, translating them.

I'm a geospatial scientist and program leader working across ArcGIS, Python, and remote sensing. My work sits between raw spatial data and the decisions it informs — turning imagery, analysis, and emerging tools into capability that teams and leaders can act on.

I hold a Master's in GIS from CSU Long Beach and a Bachelor's in Geography & GIS from CSU Sacramento, and I've volunteered with URISA GISCorps since 2021. I'm increasingly focused on where agentic AI meets geospatial work — and on helping organizations adopt it deliberately rather than reactively.

Now

Directing geospatial programs — and shaping practice beyond one office.

I lead the geospatial and remote sensing program for a U.S. Army Corps of Engineers district, setting direction for how spatial data, imagery, and analysis support engineering and emergency-response missions. That means translating complex spatial questions into clear answers for decision-makers, modernizing cloud-based GIS workflows, and mentoring staff to build capability that outlasts any single project.

Beyond the district, I lead a national community of practice on agentic AI within a federal agency — guiding how an enterprise adopts emerging AI capability in its geospatial and analytical work. It's influence that reaches across organizational lines rather than down a chain of command: setting shared direction, building consensus, and helping peers navigate a fast-moving technology deliberately.

I hold an Army-authorized small unmanned aircraft (sUAS) role supporting the district's drone program and have completed multiple USACE leadership development programs. In February 2026, my work was featured at the Esri Federal GIS Conference.

Perspective

Where I think geospatial is heading.

For decades, the hard part of geospatial work was access — getting the imagery, cleaning the data, running the analysis. That bottleneck is dissolving. Satellite and drone imagery are abundant, cloud platforms have erased most of the compute limits, and a new generation of agentic AI can carry out multi-step spatial analysis that used to demand a trained specialist and days of work.

That shifts where the value sits. The scarce skill is no longer running the analysis — it's asking the right spatial question, judging whether an answer can be trusted, and translating it into a decision someone can defend. As routine analysis becomes automated, the professionals who matter most will be the ones who set direction for these tools, keep them honest, and build the standards that let an organization adopt them without losing rigor.

My work is increasingly aimed there: not just doing the analysis, but shaping how teams and institutions do it well — deliberately, transparently, and at scale.
